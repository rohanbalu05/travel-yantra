# Mysore Palace · 5 min · en

*history*  
Look at the arch above the main gate and find the two-headed bird carved into the stone. What secrets are held by the twelve temples hidden within these walls? You are standing on ground that has swallowed three previous palaces [1], each one turning to ash before the current stone structure rose in 1912. The palace you see is the fourth to stand here, replacing a wooden structure that burned during a royal wedding in 1897 [1]. To prevent another disaster, the regent queen ordered the new building to be constructed of stone, iron, and glass instead of timber [1]. As you touch the cool, gray granite of the outer walls, you can feel the deliberate strength of a family that refused to let their home burn down again.

*legend*  
The story goes that this ground carries a heavy burden because of what happened by the water. People here say that in 1610, a widowed queen named Alamelamma fled to the Kaveri river to protect temple jewels from the new rulers of Mysuru [2]. Before jumping into the deep water, she cursed the royal family, declaring that they would never bear natural heirs [2]. For generations, the kings had to adopt their successors, and today, a small image of the queen is still kept and honored inside the palace walls to appease her spirit [2]. You can smell the sweet jasmine garlands placed before her shrine, a scent that drifts through the quiet corridors.

*history*  
Within these walls, twelve temples remain, some far older than the palace itself, standing as quiet anchors while the world outside changes [3]. Just as you arrange your own home around the kitchen or the hearth, this entire city is built to orbit this single point. Walk past the south gate and you will see the Shweta Varahaswamy temple, which has stood here since the fourteenth century [3]. You can hear the soft chiming of brass bells as local worshippers still come here every morning, treating this royal ground as a living part of their daily lives rather than a museum. The ancient stone steps of these temples are worn smooth by hundreds of years of bare feet, leading your eyes up to the carved towers that watched the old wooden palaces burn and rise again.

*history*  
A tired traveler stands on the polished granite floor of the Durbar Hall, looking up at massive cast-iron pillars that were forged in workshops across the ocean and shipped here to hold up these high ceilings [4]. You might expect this grand building, finished in 1912, to be a quiet relic of the past [4]. Yet, this palace draws a crowd that rivals and sometimes even surpasses the Taj Mahal [5]. The secret to this pull lies not in its architecture, but in how the entire city of Mysuru continues to orbit this single point [1].

*legend*  
The story goes that the living pulse of this palace even shaped what the city eats. People here say that in the royal kitchen, a cook named Kakasura Madappa was ordered to make a new sweet for the king using only gram flour, ghee, and sugar [6]. When the king asked for its name, the cook looked at the warm, buttery syrup and called it Mysore pak [6]. Today, his descendants still sell the soft, rich sweet from a small shop on Sayyaji Rao Road, where the sugary scent drifts into the street [6].

*history*  
During the autumn festival of Dasara, the palace becomes the starting line for a grand procession watched by the entire state [7]. A trained lead elephant stands before the palace gates, carrying a golden howdah that weighs close to eighty kilos [7]. To prepare for this immense weight, the animal spends months walking the city streets carrying heavy sandbags [7]. As the procession begins, you can feel the vibration in the soles of your shoes and hear the thunder of drums as the elephant steps forward, carrying the goddess Chamundeshwari [7].

*history*  
This is not a silent museum, but a living space where local families gather on Sundays to sit on the cool grass [5]. As dusk falls, close to a lakh of light bulbs flicker on, bathing the stone walls in a warm, golden glow [1]. Just as you gather around a hearth at the end of a long journey, the people of this city gather here, watching the lights shine before they return to the dark.
