# Mysore Palace · 10 min · en

*history*  
Look down at the cold grey granite beneath your feet and realize it is the fourth attempt to hold this ground [1]. You are standing on a spot that has swallowed three previous palaces, each one turning to ash before the current stone structure rose [1]. Just as you organize your own life around a single hearth, the entire city of Mysuru arranges its day around this very stone [1]. Yet, as you look at this massive fortress, you might wonder what kind of desperate emergency forced a royal family to abandon their traditional building materials forever [1].

*history*  
The answer lies in the heat of a single night in 1897, when a royal wedding turned into a disaster as the old wooden palace caught fire [1]. The family escaped the flames, but the building did not [1]. The regent queen, Kempananjammanni, decided there would be no more wooden palaces, commissioning a British architect, Henry Irwin, to build a structure of stone, iron, and glass [1]. Finished in 1912, the building was designed with Mughal-style domes, Rajput arches, and Gothic proportions [2]. As you look up at the towering stone facade, you can smell the faint scent of rain on the nearby lawns and feel the cool wind blowing from the hills, a reminder of the permanent fortress that finally conquered the threat of fire [1][2].

*history*  
You stand on the hot tarmac outside the south gate, watching an old man in a white dhoti slip off his sandals to touch the cool threshold of the Shweta Varahaswamy temple [3]. He is not here for a museum; he is stepping into the fourteenth century to buy flowers for his morning prayers [3]. Just as you navigate your own morning commute by the local train or the corner coffee shop, the people of Mysuru navigate their lives by the twelve temples standing inside these red palace walls [3]. These stone shrines are older than any royal bedroom or gilded hall you came to see [3]. Walk the dusty perimeter of the grounds and you will hear the bronze bells ringing as neighborhood residents pass through the gates for their ordinary daily worship [3]. The palace does not just sit here as a monument; its ancient gates swing open to dictate the daily heartbeat of the city. But why does a modern royal family still bow to a shadow inside these gates?

*legend*  
The answer lies in a story of stolen jewels and a desperate escape. Legend has it that in 1610, a widowed queen named Alamelamma fled to the banks of the Kaveri River at Talakad to protect her sacred temple treasures from the rising rulers of Mysuru [4]. As the soldiers closed in, people say she stood at the edge of the swirling water, spat a curse that the kings of Mysuru would never bear children, and threw herself into the river [4]. Today, the family still takes no chances with her ghost. If you walk into the palace, you can smell the sweet, heavy scent of burning jasmine incense offered before a small image of Alamelamma, whom the family still honors to ward off her ancient anger [4].

*legend*  
Look up at the arch above the main gate before you leave, and you will see the final guard against this turbulent world. The story goes that the double-headed bird carved there, the Gandaberunda, is a terrifying form the god Vishnu took to swallow a runaway divine force [5]. The kings of Mysuru used this two-headed beast as their royal emblem for centuries because it had the power to hold what no single creature could manage [5]. Today, as the sun sets, the stone bird watches over the quiet temple gates, its two heads staring out into the darkening streets of a city that still moves to its ancient command [5].

*history*  
A courtier stands shivering in the drafty left row of the Durbar Hall, waiting for a king who governs the very hours of his day [1]. Why does this empty stage of painted arches still dictate when the shops open and close in the streets outside? Do not stand in the center of the hall like the other tourists. Walk to the left-hand row of pillars to see the stage exactly as that waiting courtier did [1]. From here, look at the oil paintings of the 1930s Dasara procession lining the long corridor [6]. You can almost smell the dry dust kicked up by the painted elephants. The artists did not paint imaginary crowds; they painted real citizens of Mysuru whose great-grandchildren still recognize their faces on these walls [6]. The route those painted elephants walk is the exact path the city still clears for the procession today [6].

*history*  
Step into the Kalyana Mantapa, the grand wedding hall, where the rhythm of the city’s most intimate family milestones was once set. Look up at the ceiling where turquoise and green stained-glass peacocks filter the hot Indian sun into a cool, watery light [1]. These heavy glass panels and the cast-iron pillars holding them up were forged in the cold rains of Glasgow and shipped across the ocean [1]. Touch one of the pillars. Your fingers meet cold Scottish iron painted to look like warm, hand-carved Indian stone [1]. Just as you might decorate your own home with souvenirs of travels to make a statement to your neighbors, the Maharaja used this imported iron to show his city that Mysuru was connected to the wider world.

*history*  
The daily pulse of the city was also shaped by what happened in the palace kitchen. Here, a cook named Kakasura Madappa frantically mixed gram flour, ghee, and sugar to create a new treat for a demanding king [7]. When asked for its name, he simply called it "pak," the local word for sugar syrup [7]. Today, his descendants still run a small sweet shop on Sayyaji Rao Road [7]. If you walk there after your visit, you will find a long queue of locals waiting in the heat to buy the soft version of Mysore pak, still following the sweet rhythm started in this very kitchen [7].

*history*  
A palace electrician stands by his switchboard, his hand resting on the master lever that controls ninety-seven thousand lightbulbs [1]. He waits for the clock to strike seven, knowing that the entire city of Mysuru outside the gates has already paused, its evening traffic slowing down in anticipation of his single movement. This massive crowd outside does not gather to see a dead monument; they are waiting for this building to dictate the rhythm of their night [8][1]. Just as you might organize your own home around the warmth of a central hearth, this entire city is arranged around this single point of light [1]. Yet, as the crowd looks up at the dark, grand facade, a quiet mystery hangs over the design: why does this massive royal house turn its back on the main city gates to face a silent, dark hill to the east [6]?

*history*  
The answer to that question lies in a heavy rhythm that peaks once a year during Dasara, when a chosen elephant named Arjuna stands in the courtyard, his knees trembling under eighty kilos of pure gold [9]. Arjuna wants only to take his first steady steps forward, but his obstacle is the deafening roar of a lakh of people and the heavy scent of crushed jasmine. For months, trainers strapped heavy sandbags to his back to prepare him for this exact weight [9]. When the electrician throws his switch, a lakh of bulbs burst into life, and the golden carriage on Arjuna's back catches the glare, turning the courtyard into a sea of fire [9][1]. The procession begins, moving from this very courtyard out into the streets, proving that the palace is the beating heart that sets the city's march [9][1].

*history*  
To feel this pulse yourself without your own hunger distracting you, walk ten minutes to Mylari before the gates close to eat a soft, ghee-heavy dosa, or head to Hotel RRR near Gandhi Square for a spicy meal served on a banana leaf [6]. Then, return to the gates as the sun dips. Look east toward the dark silhouette of Chamundi Hill [6]. The palace faces this peak because the ruling family built their home to look directly at their protective goddess, Chamundeshwari, who resides at the top [6]. As the electrician finally pulls his lever and the lights die, the stone walls settle back into the shadow of the hill, waiting for the next cycle of the city's breath [6][1].
