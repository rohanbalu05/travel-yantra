# Mysore Palace · 2 min · en

*history*  
Imagine standing in the ashes of your own wedding [1]. In 1897, Regent Queen Kempananjammanni watched her wooden palace burn to the ground during a royal marriage feast [1]. Instead of fleeing the charred ruins, she hired a British architect to build a fourth palace on this exact soil—this time out of stone, iron, and glass [1]. As you stand in the crowd today, look past the grand facade to find the quiet victories of a woman who refused to let her legacy burn [1].

*legend*  
People here say the ground itself was cursed to be empty. Legend has it that in 1610, a widowed queen named Alamelamma fled from the new rulers who coveted her temple jewels [2]. She stood at the edge of the Kaveri river and cursed the dynasty to never bear children before she drowned herself [2]. To this day, the royal family honors her image inside these walls, refusing to take chances with the old curse [2].

*history*  
Walk past the gate and look up at the arch to see the double-headed bird, the Gandaberunda [3]. It is a mythical creature of immense power, yet most of the millions who crowd this courtyard—sometimes outnumbering the visitors at the Taj Mahal—walk right under it without looking up [4].

*history*  
Step into the cool, dim corridor smelling of old wax and look at the oil paintings of the 1930s Dasara procession [5]. In the wedding hall, look up at the turquoise glass peacocks [6]. They are Scottish ironwork shipped from Glasgow, pretending to be Indian stone [6].

*disputed*  
Stand at the front and look east toward the temple on Chamundi hill [7]. One view holds that the palace faces this peak because the kings believed their power was anchored directly to the goddess who watches from the summit [7]. Others argue the alignment was simply a practical choice for the grand Dasara procession to move in a straight line [7]. Either way, the two-headed bird on the gate remains fixed on the distant hill [3][7].
