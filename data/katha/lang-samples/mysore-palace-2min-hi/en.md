# Mysore Palace · 2 min · en

*legend*  
Why would a king build his home four times on the very same patch of scorched earth [1]? The story goes that in 1610, a fleeing queen named Alamelamma cursed the Wadiyar kings to never bear heirs before she drowned herself in the Kaveri river [2]. To this day, the royal family adopts its successors and honors her image inside [2]. They clung to this cursed ground, rebuilding each time it burned to prove they could survive [1]. Like anyone who has ever rebuilt a life from the ashes of a setback, they refused to yield.

*history*  
Look past the grand facade to the arch above the main gate, where a double-headed bird called the Gandaberunda sits quietly [3]. Most of the millions of visitors who crowd this courtyard—a number that rivals the Taj Mahal—walk right past it [4]. Smell the sweet jasmine worn in the hair of local families who travel here on Sunday buses [4]. They are not here for foreign monuments; they are here for their own history [4].

*history*  
Inside the long corridor, ignore the grand vistas. Look instead at the oil paintings of the 1930s Dasara procession, where local families still recognize their ancestors painted among the crowd [5]. In the Durbar Hall, do not stand in the center like everyone else. Walk to the left-hand row of pillars [6]. From here, the arches line up and the painted ceiling unrolls above you like a secret scroll [6]. This is the stage view a waiting courtier once had [6].

*history*  
In the Kalyana Mantapa wedding hall, look up at the turquoise and green stained-glass ceiling [7]. Touch the cool, green iron pillars [7]. They look like carved stone, but they are actually Scottish cast iron shipped all the way from a Glasgow foundry [7].

*history*  
This fourth palace survived because the regent queen, Kempananjammanni, ordered that no wood be used after the 1897 royal wedding fire [1]. It took fifteen years to build this stone and iron shield against fate [1]. Stand at the front and look east toward Chamundi hill [8]. The palace faces the temple of the goddess who protects it, watching the hill that never moves [8].
