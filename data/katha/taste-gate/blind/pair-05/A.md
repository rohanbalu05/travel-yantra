# Mysuru, 5 minutes

The train hissed to a halt at the Mysuru Junction, and as Maya stepped onto the platform, the frantic, high-octane hum of Bengaluru seemed to evaporate into the cool morning air. 

Mysuru did not rush. It exhaled. 

Maya had spent the last five years climbing the corporate ladder in India’s silicon valley, her life measured in deadlines, traffic jams, and paper cups of bitter espresso. But today, she was home. 

Ignoring the ride-sharing apps on her phone, Maya walked past the station gates and smiled. Standing by the curb was a traditional *tonga*—a wooden horse carriage painted in faded yellow and green. The driver, an elderly man named Khader with a white beard and a warm, crinkled smile, tipped his Nehru cap. 

"Where to, Amma?" he asked in gentle Kannada.

"Lakshmipuram, Uncle," Maya said, climbing into the carriage. 

With a soft click of Khader’s tongue and the rhythmic *clip-clop* of hooves, the journey began. Maya closed her eyes, letting the breeze wash over her. Mysuru smelled exactly as she remembered: a heady, nostalgic blend of wet earth, blooming *Mysore Mallige* (jasmine), and a faint, sweet whisper of sandalwood. 

They trotted down the wide, tree-canopied avenues. Giant rain trees arched overhead, filtering the morning sun into a mosaic of green and gold. They passed heritage bungalows with red-tiled roofs and sprawling verandas, untouched by the glass-and-steel modernity taking over the rest of the country. In the distance, the misty silhouette of Chamundi Hill stood sentinel, crowned by its ancient temple.

"The city doesn't change much, does it, Khader Uncle?" Maya asked.

Khader chuckled, gently guiding the horse around a sleepy street dog. "People try to change it, Amma. But Mysuru has a royal heart. It refuses to run when it can walk with grace."

Ten minutes later, the tonga pulled up outside a house with a yellow facade and a heavy wooden door. Standing on the porch was her grandfather, Ajja, wearing a crisp white *dhoti* and a smile that reached his eyes.

"You're late," Ajja teased, though he hugged her tightly. "The coffee is getting cold."

They sat on the veranda, the morning sun warming their faces. Ajja poured the coffee from a height, transferring the frothy, chicory-scented liquid between a brass tumbler and a *davara* bowl with the practiced ease of a master craftsman. He handed her a steel plate. On it sat two pieces of *Mysore Pak*.

Maya took a bite. The sweet was warm, porous, and heavily infused with pure ghee. It literally melted on her tongue, leaving behind the rich flavor of roasted gram flour and caramelized sugar. 

"Perfect," Maya whispered, feeling a knot of tension in her shoulders—one she hadn't even realized she was carrying—finally loosen.

"In the city, they live to work," Ajja said, sipping his coffee. "In Mysuru, we work to live. There is always time for a second cup of coffee, Maya. Remember that."

As the afternoon turned to evening, the air grew cooler, carrying the scent of rain. Maya and Ajja walked down to the heart of the city, joining a gentle stream of locals and tourists heading toward the Amba Vilas Palace. 

It was Sunday, and like every Sunday evening, the city gathered for a weekly ritual. 

They stood on the manicured lawns opposite the grand Indo-Saracenic palace. The sky turned a deep, bruised violet. The palace stood majestic but dark, its domes and arches silhouetted against the twilight. 

At exactly seven o'clock, a collective hush fell over the crowd.

Then, in a single, breathless second, ninety-seven thousand incandescent bulbs flickered to life. 

The palace erupted into a golden constellation. The brilliant, warm glow illuminated the intricate carvings, the majestic arches, and the golden dome, reflecting off the wide-open eyes of children and elders alike. It was a sight Maya had seen a hundred times growing up, yet it still made her throat tight with emotion. 

In that golden glow, Maya looked at her grandfather, then at the peaceful faces of the people around her. Nobody was checking their watches. Nobody was pushing. They were simply existing in the beauty of the moment.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket—an email notification about a Monday morning meeting. Maya looked at the screen, then slid it back into her pocket, unanswered. 

She took a deep breath of the jasmine-scented air, looked up at the glowing palace, and smiled. She was finally back in Mysuru, where time didn't fly—it lingered, enjoying the view.
