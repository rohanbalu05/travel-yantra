# Mysuru, 5 minutes

*History.* Look at the wide, straight roads stretching out from the palace gates, built not for daily traffic but as a grand stage for a single ten-day festival [1]. Mysuru carries itself like a capital because for five centuries the Wadiyar kings ruled from here or Srirangapatna [2, 8], leaving behind a city designed to hold a massive celebration and a deep wound at the same time [2]. We all have family secrets we keep behind closed doors while putting on a brave face for our guests. Why does a family that has ruled for six hundred years [3] still pay tribute to a widowed queen who jumped into a river?

*Legend.* The story goes that in 1610, a widowed queen named Alamelamma was pursued to the banks of the Kaveri at Talakad by the new rulers who wanted her temple jewels [4]. Standing by the rushing, cold water, she cursed them: let Talakad turn to sand, and let the kings of Mysuru never bear children [4]. People here say she then leapt into the river [4]. Today, dunes of sand cover the temples of Talakad, and the royal family has repeatedly relied on adoption to keep their line alive [14, 6].

*History.* While geologists attribute the sand at Talakad to wind and river shifts [4], the Wadiyar family has treated the curse seriously [4], keeping Alamelamma's image inside the palace to this day [4]. You can feel the weight of this history in the quiet corners of the grounds, smelling the faint scent of sandalwood in the air. The family has repeatedly gone sideways rather than straight down for its succession, relying on adoption to keep the line alive [4]. They did not take chances [4].

*History.* Can you look at a palace of stone, iron, and glass and see the ashes of a wedding? [5] In 1897, a single spark tore through the old wooden royal home during a marriage ceremony, leaving the regent queen, Kempananjammanni, to watch her world burn to the ground [5]. Instead of rebuilding with the same vulnerable timber, she hired British architect Henry Irwin to raise a fireproof fortress [5]. We all have moments when we must rebuild our lives from the wreckage of what we loved, choosing stronger materials for the second try. As you stand here, smell the hot ghee from the nearby Nazarbad dosa shops [6] and look at the massive stone pillars in front of you—they are a queen’s direct answer to a fire [5].

*History.* This grand building now draws visitor numbers that rival the Taj Mahal itself [7]. If you listen, you can hear the chatter of Indian families in their Sunday best, their voices echoing off the high ceilings as they show their children the grand halls [7]. Yet, this massive crowd is only half the story of a city that holds its celebrations and its wounds together [2]. Mysuru still carries itself like a quiet capital [3], but its history is split between the bright lights of the court and a dark, four-hundred-year-old family secret [2].

*Legend.* The story goes that in 1610, a widowed queen named Alamelamma was chased by the new rulers of Mysuru to the banks of the Kaveri River to surrender her temple jewels [4]. Legend has it that she stood at the water's edge, cursed the land to turn to sand, cursed the river to run with whirlpools, and declared that the kings of Mysuru would never bear heirs before she jumped into the deep water [4]. People here say that the sand dunes now burying the town of Talakad are the physical proof of her anger [6, 14]. To this day, the royal family has repeatedly had to adopt their heirs to keep the line alive [6, 14].

*History.* On Sunday nights, close to a lakh of electric bulbs come on at once, drawing the entire palace in a brilliant, blinding grid of light for a single hour [2]. This is how Mysuru celebrates its survival, a spectacular display for the public [2]. Yet, the private wound remains; inside this very palace, the family still keeps and honours an image of the cursed queen Alamelamma in the quiet shadows [4].
