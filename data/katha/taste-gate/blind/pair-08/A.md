# Devaraja Market, 5 minutes

The morning mist of Mysuru was still clinging to the grand, arched gateway of the Devaraja Market when Kabir stepped through. It was barely six o’clock, but inside, the century-old bazaar was already wide awake, breathing with the rhythmic vitality of a beast that never truly slept.

For Kabir, a travel writer weary of sterile shopping malls and digital carts, Devaraja was a pilgrimage. Built in the late 19th century during the reign of Maharaja Chamaraja Wodeyar, this market was not just a place of commerce; it was the living, beating heart of Mysuru’s heritage.

As he walked down the central aisle, the architecture immediately captured him. Timber beams, weathered by generations of monsoons, supported the high ceilings. Sunlight sliced through the skylights in dusty, golden beams, illuminating a world that felt delightfully suspended in time.

The first wave to hit him was the scent. It wasn’t a single smell, but a complex, shifting symphony. It began with the earthy, sweet aroma of wet coriander and fresh mint, transitioning abruptly into the sharp, citrusy tang of local oranges and the buttery sweetness of ripening bananas. 

"Brother! Try the Nanjangud Rasabale!" a vendor called out, gesturing to a bunch of small, plump, golden bananas. "Sweeter than honey, only found here!"

Kabir smiled, waved a polite hand, and pressed deeper into the labyrinth. 

He soon found himself in the legendary color alley. Here, the market transformed into a living canvas. Symmetrical, towering cones of *kumkum* (vermilion), turmeric, and vibrant powdered dyes—royal blue, emerald green, saffron, and magenta—stood like silent, colorful sentinels. 

Behind one of these colorful fortresses sat an elderly man named Appaji. His face was a map of deep-set wrinkles, and his fingers were permanently stained a festive shade of crimson. 

"How long have you been here, Uncle?" Kabir asked, leaning against the wooden pillar of the stall.

Appaji looked up, his eyes milky but sharp. He smiled, revealing a toothless warmth. "My grandfather sat on this very wooden plank, son. In his time, the Maharajas themselves would occasionally ride past the outer gates. The world outside has changed. Cars replaced carriages, high-rises replaced open fields. But in here? We still measure life by the kilo and the color."

Appaji reached into a sack and pulled out a small piece of natural resin. "Smell this," he said.

Kabir took it. The rich, woody scent of pure sandalwood filled his lungs. It was the smell of old Mysuru—royal, calm, and eternal. 

Moving on, the gentle hum of the market gave way to a louder, more frantic chorus. Kabir had reached the flower section. If the color alley was a painting, this was a festival. 

The air here was thick and heavy with the intoxicating fragrance of *Mysore Mallige*—the local jasmine. Hundreds of women sat on low stools, their hands moving with dizzying, practiced speed as they threaded delicate white buds onto thin jute strings. Beside them lay mounds of fiery orange marigolds, deep red roses, and purple orchids. 

A young flower seller, noticing Kabir’s camera, proudly held up a massive garland of jasmine and roses, thick enough to adorn a temple deity. "For your wife, sir?" he grinned.

"I’m just capturing the beauty," Kabir replied, snapping a photo that caught the contrast of the white jasmine against the vendor's dark, calloused hands.

"The beauty is free," the young man laughed, tossing a loose jasmine bud toward Kabir. Kabir caught it and tucked it into his shirt pocket.

By seven-thirty, the market was in full swing. The aisles were a tight squeeze of locals negotiating prices, monks in maroon robes selecting fresh ginger, and tourists staring in wide-eyed wonder. Bell-metal scales clinked, wooden crates scraped against the stone floor, and the bargaining was like a rhythmic, friendly debate.

Kabir walked out through the southern gate, stepping back onto the modern asphalt of Dhanvantri Road. The honking of scooters and the glare of glass storefronts rushed back to greet him. 

But as he walked away, he felt anchored. He reached into his pocket and touched the small jasmine bud. Its fragrance still lingered on his fingers, a sweet, fragrant reminder that amidst a rapidly changing world, the soul of Mysuru remained safely preserved under the rafters of Devaraja Market.
