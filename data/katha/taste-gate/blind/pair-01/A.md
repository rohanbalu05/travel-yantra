# Mysuru, 10 minutes

### The City of Slow Gold

The train from Bengaluru always slowed down just before it reached Mysuru, as if the engine itself was taking a deep, appreciative breath. 

For Anjana, that deceleration was the signal to close her laptop. For three years, she had lived in the hyper-speed lane of India’s Silicon Valley, where life was measured in sprint cycles, food delivery apps, and the relentless hum of traffic. But as the train crossed the Kaveri River, its waters gleaming like poured silver in the morning light, her shoulders dropped. 

When she stepped onto the platform of the Mysuru Junction, the first thing that hit her was the air. It didn’t smell of diesel and dust; it smelled of wet earth, old stone, and a faint, ghostly trace of sandalwood. 

Anjana was home. Or at least, she was back in the place that felt most like home.

She bypassed the ride-sharing lanes and walked out of the station. Waiting by the curb was a yellow-and-black auto-rickshaw, its driver, an elderly man with a thick gray mustache and a spotless white *nehru* jacket, reading a Kannada newspaper.

"Lakshmipuram, uncle," Anjana said, slipping into the back seat.

The driver looked back in the rearview mirror and smiled, a slow, unhurried expression. "*Banni,amma* (Welcome, child). No rush, right?"

"No rush," Anjana replied, smiling back. In Mysuru, rushing was considered a mild form of impoliteness.

***

The auto rattled gently along the wide, tree-canopied avenues of the city. Mysuru did not build upwards; it spread outwards like a green canopy. Massive gulmohar and rain trees arched over the roads, filtering the morning sunlight into a dappled mosaic of green and gold. They passed heritage bungalows with red-tiled roofs and whitewashed compound walls, over which purple bougainvillea spilled like wine.

Her destination was a traditional *thotti mane*—a courtyard house—where her grandfather, whom she called Ajja, still lived. 

When she entered the gate, she found him sitting on the *uyyale*, the heavy wooden swing in the veranda, reading. He was eighty-four, but his posture was as straight as the pillars of the Amba Vilas Palace.

"Anju," he said, his voice a warm rumble. He didn't stand up in a hurry; he marked his page, set his book down, and waited for her to come to him. "You look like you’ve been chased by a tiger."

"Just Bengaluru traffic, Ajja," she said, hugging him. He smelled of Pears soap and the camphor he used in his morning prayers.

"Sit," he said, nudging the swing. "First, we restore the soul. Then we talk."

Within minutes, Shanthamma, the cook who had been with the family for forty years, appeared with a steel tumbler of filter coffee. The froth was a perfect, pale-brown cloud. Anjana took a sip. It was strong, chicory-rich, and sweet enough to make her teeth ache, but it tasted like absolute safety.

Alongside the coffee was a small banana leaf holding a single, golden block of *Mysore Pak*. 

"From Guru Sweet Mart?" Anjana asked, her eyes lighting up.

"Where else?" Ajja smiled. "The descendants of the royal cook who invented it. Go on."

Anjana broke off a piece. It didn't crumble; it melted instantly on her tongue, a rich, porous burst of pure ghee, gram flour, and caramelized sugar. For a moment, the relentless mental chatter of deadlines and emails simply vanished, dissolved by a sweet that had once pleased kings.

***

By afternoon, the warmth of the day had settled over the city like a soft wool blanket. Anjana decided to walk. 

She walked toward the Devaraja Market, a century-old bazaar that was the beating heart of Mysuru’s sensory life. Entering the market was like stepping into a painting. Under the timber-framed roof, vendors sat atop wooden platforms, surrounded by pyramids of vibrant, powdered *kumkum* in shades of crimson, canary yellow, and deep orange.

Further in, the scent changed. The earthy smell of spices gave way to the intoxicating fragrance of *Mysore Mallige*—the local jasmine. Women with oiled hair stood by baskets of the tightly closed white buds, their fingers moving with impossible speed as they wove them into thick garlands using banana-fiber thread. 

Anjana bought a small string of jasmine. She wrapped it around her wrist, the warmth of her skin immediately coaxing the flowers to release their sweet, heavy perfume. 

As she walked out of the market, she heard the rhythmic *clip-clop, clip-clop* of hooves. A *tonga*, one of the city's iconic horse-drawn carriages, was passing by. Its brass lamps gleamed, and the horse, decorated with a bright red pom-pom between its ears, trotted with a proud, high-stepping gait.

On a whim, Anjana hailed it. 

"To the Palace, sahib," she told the driver, a young man who inherited the carriage from his father.

The ride was slow, a gentle sway that forced her to look at the city at a human pace. They passed the silver-domed Wellington Lodge and the majestic statue of Chamarajendra Wadiyar X, standing under a golden canopy. The driver pointed his whip toward the palace walls in the distance.

"People come from all over the world to see the palace, sister," the driver said proudly in Hindi-infused Kannada. "But they only look at the stone and the gold. They don't know that Mysuru is a feeling. You cannot take a photo of the feeling."

Anjana nodded. He was right. The city possessed an elegant, quiet dignity, a refusal to be modernized into anonymity.

***

As the sun began to dip, casting long, amber shadows across the city, Anjana met Ajja at the foot of Chamundi Hills. It was a family tradition to watch the sunset from the top. 

Instead of driving, they took the winding road up. The air grew cooler with every hairpin bend, carrying the scent of eucalyptus and dry leaves. Halfway up, they stopped at the monolithic Nandi bull. Carved out of a single boulder of black granite in the 17th century, the giant bull sat in eternal, peaceful meditation, draped in garlands of marigolds.

"Your grandmother and I used to walk up these thousand steps when we were young," Ajja said, looking out over the valley. "We would bring a flask of tea and sit right there, where the rocks overlook the lakes."

They reached the viewpoint just as the sky turned a bruised, beautiful violet, streaked with pink and gold. Below them, Mysuru lay spread out like a map of memory. She could see the dark green expanse of the Karanji and Kukkarahalli lakes, reflecting the dying light. In the center of it all, the Amba Vilas Palace stood, its grand domes and arches etched sharply against the twilight.

"Ajja," Anjana said softly, "I feel like I’m always running, but I don’t know what I’m running toward. In the city, if you stop, you get run over."

Ajja put his hand over hers. His palm was dry and warm. 

"Look at the palace, Anju," he said. "It was burned down in a fire in 1897. Completely destroyed. The royal family could have built something modern, something quick. But they waited. They hired an architect from far away, they used the best granite, the finest teak, the most patient craftsmen. It took fifteen years to rebuild."

He turned to look at her. "Beautiful things take time to build, and even longer to rebuild. You are not running out of time. You are just forgetting how to live in it."

Before she could answer, a siren wailed in the distance. It was precisely 7:00 PM on a Sunday.

Instantly, the dark silhouette of the palace below them erupted into flame. 

Not real fire, but the warm, golden glow of nearly one hundred thousand incandescent lightbulbs. It didn't happen in a harsh, sudden flash; it was a blooming of light, a breathtaking constellation of gold that defined every arch, pillar, and dome against the dark night sky.

The crowd around them gasped in unison. It was a sight Anjana had seen dozens of times in her childhood, yet it never failed to tighten her throat with a sudden, clean emotion. The palace looked like a jewel box left open on a dark velvet cloth.

"They tried to change these bulbs to LEDs a few years ago," Ajja murmured, his eyes reflecting the distant gold. "But the people protested. LEDs are too bright, too cold. We wanted the old bulbs. We wanted the warm, slow light."

Anjana looked down at the glowing city. The jasmine on her wrist caught the cool mountain breeze, releasing one final, fragrant sigh. 

She felt the tight knot in her chest—the one she had carried for three years—finally begin to unravel. She didn't need to find all the answers tonight. She didn't need to solve her life by Monday morning. 

Like the city below her, she just needed to glow with her own warm, patient light.

"Let's go down, Ajja," Anjana said, slipping her arm through his. "I want to walk through the palace gates while the lights are still on."

"No rush," Ajja smiled, turning back toward the path. 

"No rush at all," she agreed.
